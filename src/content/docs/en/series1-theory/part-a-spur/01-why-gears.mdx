## 1. Introduction: Evolution of Power Transmission Elements and Limitations of Friction Wheels

The most primitive and intuitive mechanical device for transmitting rotational power from one shaft to another is a pair of smooth cylinders pressed against each other, relying on contact friction—known as **Friction Wheels**.

The operational principle of friction wheels is straightforward. Two cylindrical wheels of radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ are mounted on shafts separated by center distance $a$, and an external normal clamping force $F_N$ is applied to press them firmly together. When the driving shaft rotates at angular velocity $\omega_1$, with static friction coefficient $\mu_s$ at the contact interface, the maximum transferable tangential force $F_{t,\max}$ governed by Coulomb friction is:

$$F_t \le \mu_s F_N$$

The maximum torque transferable by the driving shaft is:

$$T_{1,\max} = r_1 \cdot F_{t,\max} = \mu_s r_1 F_N$$

```
[Drive Shaft O₁ (Radius r₁)] ──(Normal Force FN)──▶ [Contact Point P (Ft ≤ μ FN)] ──(Driven)──▶ [Driven Shaft O₂ (Radius r₂)]
```

In an ideal pure-rolling state without slip, the pitch line velocity is identical, giving a constant transmission ratio $i$:

$$v = r_1 \omega_1 = r_2 \omega_2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad i = \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \mathrm{Constant}$$

![Figure 1A: Power Transmission Mechanism of Friction Wheels](/images/part1/en/fig1a_friction_drive.png)

However, in practical mechanical systems, friction wheels suffer from critical mechanical limitations:

1. **Slip and Loss of Phase Synchronization**: When transmitted torque $T_1$ exceeds friction limit torque $\mu_s r_1 F_N$, or when oil contamination, moisture, or surface wear reduces the friction coefficient, immediate slipping occurs. Consequently, the speed ratio $i(t)$ fluctuates unpredictably, making friction drives completely unsuitable for phase-synchronized machinery.
2. **Excessive Bearing Loads and Shaft Bending**: To transmit tangential force $F_t$ reliably without slip, Coulomb friction requires a minimum continuous normal clamping force $F_N \ge F_t / \mu_s$. Quantifying this for typical steel-on-steel boundary lubricated contacts ($\mu_s \approx 0.15$):
$$F_N \ge \frac{F_t}{\mu_s} = \frac{F_t}{0.15} \approx 6.67 F_t \approx 6.7 F_t$$
This means an enormous clamping force of **approximately 6.7 times the useful tangential force** must be continuously exerted on the shafts and bearings. By comparison, in a standard involute gear with reference pressure angle $\alpha = 20^\circ$, the shaft separating radial force is $F_r = F_t \tan 20^\circ \approx 0.364 F_t$, resulting in a drastic bearing load disparity:
$$\frac{F_{N,\mathrm{friction}}}{F_{r,\mathrm{gear}}} = \frac{6.67 F_t}{0.364 F_t} \approx 18.3$$
The bearing load in friction drives is over 18 times higher than that of equivalent involute gears, causing premature bearing fatigue failure and requiring massive housing structures.
3. **Frictional Wear and Thermal Damage**: As soon as slip occurs, severe frictional heating develops at the contact line, leading to uneven surface wear and a catastrophic drop in transmission efficiency.

## 2. The Need for Tooth Form Generation: Limitations of Notched Cylinders and Dynamic Pulsation

To prevent slipping, one might simply cut rectangular or semicircular notches into the cylinder rims to create interlocking teeth. These are known as **simple notched cylinders**.

However, simply creating interlocking teeth does not solve the problem. Arbitrary profiles that are not mathematically formulated induce severe dynamic catastrophes.

When two arbitrary curved surfaces mesh and rotate, the contact point $K$ continually shifts. If the common normal at the contact point swings periodically across the line of centers, the instantaneous transmission ratio $i(t) = \omega_1(t)/\omega_2(t)$ oscillates:

$$i(t) = \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2(t)} \ne \mathrm{Constant}$$

When the driving motor rotates at constant angular velocity $\omega_1$, letting the drive shaft rotation angle be $\theta_1(t)$ ($d\theta_1/dt = \omega_1$), the driven shaft angular velocity is $\omega_2(t) = \omega_1 / i(\theta_1(t))$. Differentiating with respect to time $t$ via the chain rule yields:

$$\alpha_2(t) = \frac{d\omega_2}{dt} = \frac{d\omega_2}{d\theta_1} \frac{d\theta_1}{dt} = \frac{d}{d\theta_1}\left(\frac{\omega_1}{i(\theta_1)}\right) \cdot \omega_1 = -\frac{\omega_1^2}{[i(\theta_1)]^2} \frac{di(\theta_1)}{d\theta_1} \ne 0$$

Whenever $di/d\theta_1 \ne 0$, a non-zero angular acceleration $\alpha_2(t)$ is inevitably generated on the driven shaft. Crucially, this angular acceleration scales with the **square of the operating speed ($\omega_1^2$)**. By Newton's second law, with load inertia $I$ and pitch radius $r_2$, dynamic torque $T_{dyn}(t)$ and normal contact force $F_{dyn}(t)$ are generated:

$$T_{dyn}(t) = I \cdot \alpha_2(t) = -I \frac{\omega_1^2}{[i(\theta_1)]^2} \frac{di(\theta_1)}{d\theta_1}$$

$$F_{dyn}(t) = \frac{T_{dyn}(t)}{r_2} = -I \frac{\omega_1^2}{r_2 [i(\theta_1)]^2} \frac{di(\theta_1)}{d\theta_1}$$

This dynamic shock force repeatedly impacts tooth flanks at frequencies from tens to hundreds of hertz, causing severe NVH and tooth root fracture. Therefore, gear engineering is fundamentally about finding **a special mathematical curve that guarantees strictly zero angular acceleration ($d\omega_2/dt \equiv 0$) throughout mesh engagement**.

## 3. Genesis of the Involute Curve: Geometry Unwinding from a Cylinder

The ideal tooth curve was first mathematically explored by French mathematician Philippe de La Hire (1640–1718) in 1694 (*Traité des épicycloïdes*), and systematically formulated in analytical geometry by Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) in 1760: the **Involute of a Circle**.

The concept of the involute curve is remarkably intuitive. Imagine a taut string wrapped around a cylinder of radius $r_b$ (the **Base Circle**). As the string unwinds while kept strictly taut, its end traces out an involute curve.

```
                  Base Circle (Radius rb)
                     . - ~ - .
                 .               .
               .                   .
              .         O           .
               .                   .  Unwinding Taut String (Length L = rb · θ)
                 .               .     \
                   ' - _ _ _ - ' ───────▶ K (Tracing Involute Curve)
```

The 3 fundamental invariants of the involute curve are:

1. **String Length Equals Arc Length**:
   $$L = r_b \cdot \theta$$
2. **The String is the Curve Normal**: The taut string is perpendicular to the tangent of the involute at point $K$, acting as the curve normal vector.
3. **All Normals are Tangent to the Base Circle**: Radius of curvature $\rho_K$ strictly equals the unwound length:
   $$\rho_K = r_b \cdot \theta$$

With origin at base circle center $O$ and starting unwinding point on the $x$-axis, the parametric equations of point $K(x_K, y_K)$ are:

$$x_K = r_b (\cos\theta + \theta \sin\theta)$$

$$y_K = r_b (\sin\theta - \theta \cos\theta)$$

At radius $r_K$ with pressure angle $\alpha_K$:

$$r_b = r_K \cos\alpha_K \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \cos\alpha_K = \frac{r_b}{r_K}$$

$$\theta = \tan\alpha_K$$

$$\mathrm{inv}\,\alpha_K = \tan\alpha_K - \alpha_K$$

Note: Pressure angle $\alpha_K$ must strictly be in radians.

## 4. Fundamental Law of Gearing and Proof of Camus's Theorem

The kinematic condition for constant angular velocity ratio is the **Law of Gearing** (or **Willis's Law**). Building on Philippe de La Hire's 1694 foundation, it was geometrically formalized by French mathematician Charles Camus in 1733 and later popularized by Robert Willis in 1838.

![Figure 2: Geometric Proof of Willis's Law of Gearing](/images/part1/en/fig2_willis_law_geometry.png)

### 4.1 Kinematic Contact Condition: Velocity Normal Equivalence

At contact point $K$ between gear 1 and gear 2, with velocities $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$:

$$v_1 = \omega_1 \cdot O_1 K, \quad v_2 = \omega_2 \cdot O_2 K$$

![Figure 3: Contact Point Kinematics and Velocity Vector Decomposition](/images/part1/en/fig3_contact_velocity_vectors.png)

To prevent penetration and tooth separation, normal velocity components along common normal $n$-$n$ must match:

$$v_{n1} = v_{n2}$$

The velocity difference in the tangential $t$-$t$ direction defines the relative sliding velocity ($v_g$ or $v_s$):

$$v_g = v_{t1} - v_{t2}$$

Sliding velocity $v_g = v_{t1} - v_{t2}$ equals zero (Pure Rolling) **only at pitch point $P$ ($K = P$)**, where $v_{t1} = v_{t2}$. At all other contact positions along the line of action ($K \ne P$), tangential velocities differ, inevitably creating relative sliding ($v_g \ne 0$). Specifically, the tangential velocity of each flank is proportional to its radius of curvature, given by $v_{t1} = \omega_1 \cdot N_1 K = \omega_1 (N_1 P + PK)$ and $v_{t2} = \omega_2 \cdot N_2 K = \omega_2 (N_2 P - PK)$. Since $\omega_1 \cdot N_1 P = \omega_2 \cdot N_2 P$ cancels at pitch point $P$, the sliding velocity scales strictly with distance $PK$:

$$v_g(K) = (\omega_1 + \omega_2) \cdot PK$$

Therefore, involute gear meshing is not "slip-free everywhere" but a compound motion of rolling and sliding (Rolling with Sliding). Elastohydrodynamic lubrication (EHL) and proper backlash are essential to prevent adhesive scuffing.

### 4.2 Proof via Similar Triangles

Dropping perpendiculars $O_1 N_1$ and $O_2 N_2$ from centers onto normal $n$-$n$:

$$v_{n1} = v_1 \cos\beta_1 = (\omega_1 \cdot O_1 K) \cdot \frac{O_1 N_1}{O_1 K} = \omega_1 \cdot O_1 N_1$$

$$v_{n2} = v_2 \cos\beta_2 = (\omega_2 \cdot O_2 K) \cdot \frac{O_2 N_2}{O_2 K} = \omega_2 \cdot O_2 N_2$$

Equating normal velocities:

$$\omega_1 \cdot O_1 N_1 = \omega_2 \cdot O_2 N_2$$

$$\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{O_2 N_2}{O_1 N_1}$$

Intersecting normal $n$-$n$ with line of centers $O_1 O_2$ at pitch point $P$:

$$\Delta O_1 N_1 P \sim \Delta O_2 N_2 P$$

$$\frac{O_2 N_2}{O_1 N_1} = \frac{O_2 P}{O_1 P}$$

$$\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{O_2 P}{O_1 P} = \mathrm{Constant}$$

> **Law of Gearing (Willis's Law)**  
> "A constant angular velocity ratio requires that the common normal to the tooth profiles at all contact points must pass through a fixed point (Pitch Point $P$) on the line of centers."

### 4.3 Center Distance Invariance of Involute Gears

Unlike cycloidal gears, involute gears possess **center distance invariance**:
1. All normals to an involute must be tangent to its base circle.
2. The common normal (Common Normal) between two involutes in space is uniquely fixed along the common internal tangent to the two base circles, which forms the **Line of Action (LOA)** along which contact moves and transmits normal force.
3. Pitch point $P$ divides center distance strictly in the ratio $r_{w2}/r_{w1}$:
   $$\frac{O_2 P}{O_1 P} = \frac{r_{w2}}{r_{w1}} = \frac{r_{b2}}{r_{b1}} = \mathrm{Constant}$$

Circles passing through operating pitch point $P$ are **Working Pitch Circles ($d_{w1}, d_{w2}$)**, related to standard reference pitch diameters ($d_1 = mz_1, d_2 = mz_2$) by:

$$d_{w1} = \frac{2 a'}{u + 1} = d_1 \frac{\cos\alpha}{\cos\alpha_w}, \quad d_{w2} = u \cdot d_{w1} = d_2 \frac{\cos\alpha}{\cos\alpha_w}$$

Only at standard center distance ($a' = a$) do working pitch circles coincide with reference pitch circles ($d_{w1} = d_1, d_{w2} = d_2, \alpha_w = \alpha$). If center distance increases to $a'$:
1. The line of action remains a straight line along the base circle common internal tangent.
2. Working pressure angle increases from $\alpha$ to $\alpha_w$ ($\cos\alpha_w = a \cos\alpha / a'$).
3. Velocity ratio remains strictly constant at $\omega_1/\omega_2 = r_{b2}/r_{b1} = z_2/z_1$ with $0.000000$ deviation.

## 5. Three Fundamental Design Parameters: z, α, m

### 5.1 Number of Teeth (z)

In gear drives, angular velocity ratio is the **Transmission Ratio ($i = \omega_1 / \omega_2 = n_1 / n_2$)**, while the ratio of teeth is the **Gear Ratio ($u = z_2 / z_1 \ge 1.0$)**:

$$i = \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{n_1}{n_2}$$

$$u = \frac{z_2}{z_1} \ge 1.0$$

Under ISO 6336 and DIN 3960, tooth contact stress and bending strength standards strictly require $u \ge 1.0$. For pinion-driven reduction gearboxes, $i = u$. In speed-increasing drives where the large gear drives the pinion, transmission ratio is $i = 1/u < 1.0$, whereas gear ratio $u$ under ISO 6336 is always defined by standard as $u = z_2 / z_1 \ge 1.0$.
Standard full-depth gears require $z \ge 17$ at $\alpha = 20^\circ$ to prevent **undercutting**.

### 5.2 Standard Pressure Angle (α)

ISO 53, KS B 0102, and DIN 867 adopt $\alpha = 20^\circ$ as global standard:

$$F_t = F_n \cos\alpha$$

$$F_r = F_n \sin\alpha = F_t \tan\alpha$$

- **Larger pressure angle ($\alpha = 25^\circ$)**: Thicker tooth root, higher bending capacity, but higher radial separating force $F_r$.
- **Smaller pressure angle ($\alpha = 14.5^\circ$)**: Lower radial force on bearings, but thinner root and increased risk of undercut.

### 5.3 Module (m) and Circular Pitch (p)

Module represents tooth size (mm):

$$m = \frac{d}{z} \quad [\mathrm{mm}]$$

Circular pitch $p$:

$$p = \frac{\pi d}{z} = \pi \cdot m \quad [\mathrm{mm}]$$

Standard Module Series (ISO 54 / KS B 0102):

| Series | Standard Module $m$ [mm] |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Series 1 (Preferred)** | 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, 12.0, 16.0, 20.0 |
| **Series 2 (Secondary)** | 0.7, 0.9, 1.75, 2.25, 2.75, 3.5, 4.5, 5.5, 7.0, 9.0, 11.0, 14.0, 18.0 |

### 5.4 Standard Spur Geometry Equations

Standard full-depth spur gears (ISO 53 / DIN 867: $h_a^* = 1.0$, $c^* = 0.25$):

1. **Reference Pitch Diameter**:
   $$d = m \cdot z$$
2. **Base Diameter**:
   $$d_b = d \cdot \cos\alpha = m \cdot z \cdot \cos\alpha$$
3. **Tip Diameter**:
   $$d_a = d + 2h_a = d + 2m = m(z + 2)$$
4. **Root Diameter**:
   $$d_f = d - 2h_f = d - 2(1 + c^*)m = m(z - 2.5)$$
5. **Standard Center Distance**:
   $$a = \frac{d_1 + d_2}{2} = \frac{m(z_1 + z_2)}{2}$$
6. **Tooth Thickness & Space Width**:
   $$s = e = \frac{p}{2} = \frac{\pi m}{2}$$

In practical gear systems, **circumferential backlash ($j_t > 0$)** is mandatory to accommodate thermal expansion, manufacturing tolerances, and lubricant film passage. Therefore, $s = \pi m / 2$ is the nominal reference tooth thickness before backlash deduction.

### 6.2 Hand Calculation Example

**[Design Specifications]**
- Module: $m = 4.0\text{ mm}$
- Pressure Angle: $\alpha = 20.0^\circ$ ($\cos 20^\circ \approx 0.9396926$)
- Pinion Teeth: $z_1 = 19$
- Gear Teeth: $z_2 = 57$
- Profile Shift: $x_1 = x_2 = 0.000$

---

#### Step 1: Gear Ratio and Circular Pitch
- Gear Ratio (Tooth Ratio $u$):
  $$u = \frac{z_2}{z_1} = \frac{57}{19} = 3.000000$$
  (For a pinion-driven speed reducer, transmission ratio $i = u = 3.000000$)
- Circular Pitch:
  $$p = \pi \cdot m = 3.14159265 \times 4.0 = 12.566371\text{ mm}$$
- Tooth Thickness and Space Width:
  $$s = e = \frac{p}{2} = \frac{12.566371}{2} = 6.283185\text{ mm}$$

#### Step 2: Pitch and Base Diameters
- Pinion Pitch Diameter:
  $$d_1 = m \cdot z_1 = 4.0 \times 19 = 76.000000\text{ mm}$$
- Gear Pitch Diameter:
  $$d_2 = m \cdot z_2 = 4.0 \times 57 = 228.000000\text{ mm}$$
- Pinion Base Diameter:
  $$d_{b1} = d_1 \cos 20^\circ = 76.0 \times 0.93969262 = 71.416639\text{ mm}$$
- Gear Base Diameter:
  $$d_{b2} = d_2 \cos 20^\circ = 228.0 \times 0.93969262 = 214.249918\text{ mm}$$

#### Step 3: Tip and Root Diameters
- Addendum: $h_a = 1.0 \times m = 4.000000\text{ mm}$
- Dedendum: $h_f = 1.25 \times m = 5.000000\text{ mm}$
- Whole Depth: $h = h_a + h_f = 9.000000\text{ mm}$
- Pinion Tip Diameter:
  $$d_{a1} = d_1 + 2h_a = 76.0 + 2(4.0) = 84.000000\text{ mm}$$
- Gear Tip Diameter:
  $$d_{a2} = d_2 + 2h_a = 228.0 + 2(4.0) = 236.000000\text{ mm}$$
- Pinion Root Diameter:
  $$d_{f1} = d_1 - 2h_f = 76.0 - 2(5.0) = 66.000000\text{ mm}$$
- Gear Root Diameter:
  $$d_{f2} = d_2 - 2h_f = 228.0 - 2(5.0) = 218.000000\text{ mm}$$

#### Step 4: Center Distance and Radius Ratio Verification
- Standard Center Distance:
  $$a = \frac{d_1 + d_2}{2} = \frac{76.000000 + 228.000000}{2} = 152.000000\text{ mm}$$
- Base Radius Ratio vs Pitch Radius Ratio Cross-Check:
  $$\frac{r_{b2}}{r_{b1}} = \frac{214.249918 / 2}{71.416639 / 2} = \frac{107.124959}{35.708320} = 3.000000$$
  $$\frac{r_2}{r_1} = \frac{114.000000}{38.000000} = 3.000000$$

The deviation between base radius ratio ($r_{b2}/r_{b1}$) and reference pitch radius ratio ($r_2/r_1$) is exactly **$0.000000$** (dimensionless), perfectly matching the working pitch radius ratio ($r_{w2}/r_{w1}$) at standard center distance and confirming the strictly constant ratio $i = u = 3.000000$ via involute geometry.
