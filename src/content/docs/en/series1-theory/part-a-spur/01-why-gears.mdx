## 1. Introduction: Evolution of Power Transmission Elements and Limitations of Friction Wheels

The most primitive mechanical device for transmitting rotational power from one shaft to another is a pair of smooth cylinders pressed against each other, relying on contact friction—known as **Friction Wheels**.

When two cylindrical wheels of radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ are mounted on shafts separated by center distance $a$ and pressed together with normal force $F_N$, the maximum transferable tangential force $F_{t,\max}$ governed by Coulomb friction is:

$$F_t \le \mu_s F_N$$

The maximum torque transferable by the driving shaft is:

$$T_{1,\max} = r_1 \cdot F_{t,\max} = \mu_s r_1 F_N$$

```
[Drive Shaft O₁ (Radius r₁)] ──(Normal Force FN)──▶ [Contact Point P (Ft ≤ μ FN)] ──(Driven)──▶ [Driven Shaft O₂ (Radius r₂)]
```

In an ideal pure-rolling state without slip, the pitch line velocity is identical, giving a constant velocity ratio $u$:

$$v = r_1 \omega_1 = r_2 \omega_2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad u = \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \mathrm{Constant}$$

![Figure 1A: Power Transmission Mechanism of Friction Wheels](/images/part1/en/fig1a_friction_drive.png)

However, friction wheels suffer from critical mechanical limitations:
1. **Slip and Loss of Phase Synchronization**: When transmitted torque exceeds friction capacity or oil contamination lowers friction, slipping occurs, destroying constant angular velocity ratio $u(t)$.
2. **Excessive Bearing Loads**: High torque transmission requires enormous normal clamp forces $F_N$, causing severe bending moments and premature bearing fatigue.
3. **Frictional Wear and Heating**: Slip induces intense local heating and uneven surface wear.

### Friction Wheels vs Involute Gear Drive Comparison

![Figure 1B: Form-Closed Conjugate Meshing of Involute Gears](/images/part1/en/fig1b_gear_drive.png)

| Comparison Item | Friction Wheels | Involute Gear Drive |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

## 2. The Need for Tooth Form Generation: Limitations of Notched Cylinders and Dynamic Pulsation

To prevent slipping, one might simply cut rectangular or semicircular notches into the cylinder rims. However, arbitrary tooth shapes cause a dynamic catastrophe.

When arbitrary profiles mesh, the common normal at the contact point swings periodically across the center line. As a result, the instantaneous speed ratio $u(t) = \omega_1(t)/\omega_2(t)$ oscillates:

$$u(t) = \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2(t)} \ne \mathrm{Constant}$$

Even if the driving shaft rotates at constant $\omega_1$, the driven shaft experiences angular acceleration:

$$\alpha_2(t) = \frac{d\omega_2(t)}{dt} \ne 0$$

By Newton's second law, with load inertia $I$, huge dynamic impact forces are generated:

$$F_{dyn}(t) = I \cdot \alpha_2(t) = I \cdot \frac{d\omega_2(t)}{dt}$$

This causes severe noise, vibration, and tooth breakage. Therefore, gear engineering is fundamentally about finding **a special mathematical curve that guarantees strictly zero angular acceleration ($d\omega_2/dt \equiv 0$) throughout mesh engagement**.

## 3. Genesis of the Involute Curve: Geometry Unwinding from a Cylinder

The ideal tooth curve was formulated by Leonhard Euler: the **Involute of a Circle**.

Imagine a taut string wrapped around a cylinder of radius $r_b$ (the **Base Circle**). As the string unwinds while kept taut, its end traces out an involute curve.

```
                  Base Circle (Radius rb)
                     . - ~ - .
                 .               .
               .                   .
              .         O           .
               .                   .  Unwinding Taut String (Length L = rb · θ)
                 .               .     \
                   ' - _ _ _ - ' ───────▶ K (Tracing Involute Curve)
```

The 3 fundamental invariants of the involute curve are:
1. **String Length Equals Arc Length**:
   $$L = r_b \cdot \theta$$
2. **The String is the Curve Normal**: The taut string is perpendicular to the tangent of the involute at point $K$, acting as the curve normal.
3. **All Normals are Tangent to the Base Circle**: Radius of curvature $\rho_K$ equals the unwound length:
   $$\rho_K = r_b \cdot \theta$$

Parametric equations with base radius $r_b$ and roll angle $\theta$:

$$x_K = r_b (\cos\theta + \theta \sin\theta)$$

$$y_K = r_b (\sin\theta - \theta \cos\theta)$$

At radius $r_K$ with pressure angle $\alpha_K$:

$$r_b = r_K \cos\alpha_K \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \cos\alpha_K = \frac{r_b}{r_K}$$

$$\theta = \tan\alpha_K$$

$$\mathrm{inv}\,\alpha_K = \tan\alpha_K - \alpha_K$$

## 4. Fundamental Law of Gearing and Proof of Camus's Theorem

The kinematic condition for constant angular velocity ratio is the **Law of Gearing** (or **Willis's Law**), formalized by Charles Camus.

![Figure 2: Geometric Proof of Willis's Law of Gearing](/images/part1/en/fig2_willis_law_geometry.png)

### 4.1 Kinematic Contact Condition: Velocity Normal Equivalence

At contact point $K$ between gear 1 and gear 2, with velocities $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$:

$$v_1 = \omega_1 \cdot O_1 K, \quad v_2 = \omega_2 \cdot O_2 K$$

![Figure 3: Contact Point Kinematics and Velocity Vector Decomposition](/images/part1/en/fig3_contact_velocity_vectors.png)

To prevent penetration and tooth separation, normal velocity components along common normal $n$-$n$ must match:

$$v_{n1} = v_{n2}$$

Tangential difference is sliding velocity $v_g$:

$$v_g = v_{t1} - v_{t2}$$

### 4.2 Proof via Similar Triangles

Dropping perpendiculars $O_1 N_1$ and $O_2 N_2$ from centers onto normal $n$-$n$:

$$v_{n1} = v_1 \cos\beta_1 = (\omega_1 \cdot O_1 K) \cdot \frac{O_1 N_1}{O_1 K} = \omega_1 \cdot O_1 N_1$$

$$v_{n2} = v_2 \cos\beta_2 = (\omega_2 \cdot O_2 K) \cdot \frac{O_2 N_2}{O_2 K} = \omega_2 \cdot O_2 N_2$$

Equating normal velocities:

$$\omega_1 \cdot O_1 N_1 = \omega_2 \cdot O_2 N_2$$

$$\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{O_2 N_2}{O_1 N_1}$$

Intersecting normal $n$-$n$ with line of centers $O_1 O_2$ at pitch point $P$:

$$\Delta O_1 N_1 P \sim \Delta O_2 N_2 P$$

$$\frac{O_2 N_2}{O_1 N_1} = \frac{O_2 P}{O_1 P}$$

$$\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{O_2 P}{O_1 P} = \mathrm{Constant}$$

> **Law of Gearing (Willis's Law)**  
> "A constant angular velocity ratio requires that the common normal to the tooth profiles at all contact points must pass through a fixed point (Pitch Point $P$) on the line of centers."

### 4.3 Center Distance Invariance of Involute Gears

Unlike cycloidal gears, involute gears possess **center distance invariance**:
1. All normals to an involute must be tangent to its base circle.
2. The common normal between two involutes is the unique common tangent to the two base circles.
3. Pitch point $P$ divides center distance strictly in the ratio $r_{b2}/r_{b1}$:
   $$\frac{O_2 P}{O_1 P} = \frac{r_{b2}}{r_{b1}} = \mathrm{Constant}$$
Even if center distance $a$ varies due to manufacturing tolerances or thermal growth, the velocity ratio remains exactly constant ($0.000000$ deviation).

## 5. Three Fundamental Design Parameters: z, α, m

### 5.1 Number of Teeth (z)

Ratio of teeth dictates transmission ratio:

$$u = \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{z_2}{z_1}$$

Standard full-depth gears require $z \ge 17$ at $\alpha = 20^\circ$ to avoid undercutting.

### 5.2 Standard Pressure Angle (α)

ISO 53 and global standards adopt $\alpha = 20^\circ$:

$$F_t = F_n \cos\alpha, \quad F_r = F_n \sin\alpha = F_t \tan\alpha$$

### 5.3 Module (m) and Circular Pitch (p)

Module represents tooth size (mm):

$$m = \frac{d}{z} \quad [\mathrm{mm}]$$

Circular pitch $p$:

$$p = \frac{\pi d}{z} = \pi \cdot m \quad [\mathrm{mm}]$$

Standard Module Series (ISO 54 / KS B 0102):
- **Series 1**: 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, 12.0, 16.0, 20.0 mm.

### 5.4 Standard Spur Geometry Equations

1. Pitch Diameter: $d = m \cdot z$
2. Base Diameter: $d_b = d \cos\alpha = m z \cos\alpha$
3. Tip Diameter: $d_a = d + 2h_a = m(z + 2)$
4. Root Diameter: $d_f = d - 2h_f = m(z - 2.5)$
5. Standard Center Distance: $a = m(z_1 + z_2)/2$
6. Tooth Thickness: $s = \pi m / 2$
